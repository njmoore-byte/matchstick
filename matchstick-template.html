The Algorithmic Consumer · Volume One

The algorithm
has a long memory.

Why "newness" is no longer the peak of the curve — and what that means for every business unit at Matrix.

Words by Nathan · Insights & Education

In 2022, Kate Bush's Running Up That Hill hit number one in the UK. The song had been released 37 years earlier. Streams jumped 8,700% in weeks. Bush became the oldest female artist ever to top the UK Singles Chart — at 63, with a track most of her new listeners hadn't been born to hear the first time.

There was no marketing campaign. No re-release. No new album. Just a Netflix scene, a TikTok clip, and an algorithm that doesn't care when a song was made.

This is the most important shift in consumer behaviour you're probably not planning around.

Part One

The end of "new"

For most of broadcast and retail history, mass culture has run on a chronological clock. Songs charted on release. Magazines sold the month they printed. Beauty brands launched, peaked, and decayed in a tidy 6-to-12-month curve. Recency was relevance.

For You changed that. TikTok's algorithm has no temporal bias — only an engagement bias. A 1985 track and a song released last Tuesday compete on identical terms. Whichever resonates wins; release date is a footnote.

The launch lights the fuse. The algorithm decides when it explodes.

The implications cascade. Fleetwood Mac's Dreams (1977) re-entered the Billboard chart 43 years after release. Brenda Lee hit number one on the Hot 100 at 79, with a song from 1958 — breaking the record for the longest gap between release and chart-topper. Glass Animals' Heat Waves took 59 weeks to climb to number one, the slowest ascent in Billboard history. Warner Music has built a team whose explicit job is mining its back catalogue for tracks the algorithm might wake up.

This isn't nostalgia. The money in music catalogues — Queen's sold to Sony for $1.27bn, Springsteen's for $500m, Dylan's for around $500m — only makes sense if you believe a 50-year-old recording will keep finding new audiences indefinitely. Investors aren't betting on memory. They're betting on the algorithm.

From the front

Beauty is doing the same thing — quietly.

The archive-mining playbook is now an industry-wide strategy. Lancôme has rebooted Juicy Tubes twice (2020, 2024) and built its 25th-anniversary campaign around Paris Hilton, Hilary Duff, Ed Westwick and Kelly Rowland reminiscing about the 2000s.

Clinique's Almost Lipstick in Black Honey — launched in 1971 — has amassed over 240 million TikTok views, with one tube selling every three minutes after Liv Tyler's Lord of the Rings footage went viral two decades after the film. MAC has turned 90s archive drops into a seasonal calendar event. The Ordinary just relaunched a discontinued foundation because the algorithm wouldn't stop asking for it.

Sol de Janeiro is the cleanest analogue. Brazilian Bum Bum Cream launched in 2015 and was a quiet niche product for six years before TikTok turned it into a billion-dollar brand. The formula didn't change. The algorithm did.

The new makes noise. The catalogue makes echoes.

What this means · 01

Cowshed

Today's refresh isn't a reset — it's an ignition point. The brand carries 25+ years of heritage: Babington, the original spa, the Soho House DNA. In the algorithmic age, a refresh activates the back catalogue. Every "have you tried Cowshed?" video that follows today is a free distribution channel for two and a half decades of meaning. The storytelling should be deliberately layered — new at the front, heritage running through it.

What this means · 02

Private Brands

Past is a strategic place to mine. Build a dormant SKU register. Audit what we've discontinued in the last five years and ask which products had emotional signatures worth reviving — reformulated for today's regulations, but emotionally faithful. What's already had a moment? is now a legitimate NPD brief, not a backwards-facing one.

What this means · 03

Travel

The premium travel consumer skews older and less online — but they're not insulated. In a category where every challenger brand looks freshly minted, heritage becomes the cut-through signal. TAP × Benamôr (1925). ANA × L'Occitane × Globe-Trotter (120 years). Finnair × Marimekko (1960s prints). Qantas built its Centenary Collection around six kits inspired by the airline's own iconic design eras. Singapore × Penhaligon's. Cathay × Bamford.

The pattern is unmistakable: premium buyers are choosing partners whose value lies precisely in their endurance. Don't pitch the newest, slickest formulator. Pitch the partner whose archive, design language and longevity signal something a challenger can't fake — and consider retro-led design as a category strategy, not a nostalgia gimmick.

Heritage isn't a tax on a brand. It's an asset on the balance sheet.

The Bottom Line

The decay curve is dead. So is the assumption that a product's commercial peak is the moment it launches. Brands that treat their back catalogue as a portfolio of dormant options — rather than a graveyard of expired SKUs — will win the next decade. Kate Bush sold 8,700% more of a song she wrote in 1985 without lifting a finger. The question for every brand at Matrix is what's sitting in our archive waiting to do the same.
